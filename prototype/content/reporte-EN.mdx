## 01 — Abstract

### The hidden tax of modern computing.

A data center is a stack of three negotiated contracts: power with the utility, racks with the IT operator, and scheduling with the workload owner. Each layer is provisioned for the peak capacity of the layer above, and each measures utilization based on its own denominator. The result is a compound overhead that has never been named, measured, or priced as a single phenomenon.

This report introduces the **Stranded Capacity Index (SCI)**, a taxonomy of nine named failure modes distributed across three physical layers. The taxonomy aims to be a shared vocabulary for facility operators, IT architects, and workload schedulers — three communities whose incentives are currently misaligned.

## 02 — Introduction

### Why this report exists.

<div className="dropcap text-[17px] leading-[1.75] text-[var(--ink)] mb-6 block">
Over the last decade, the conversation about data center efficiency has been dominated by a single metric: PUE. PUE tells you how much of the power you purchased actually reached the servers. It does not tell you if those servers did anything useful. As density rises, GPU clusters replace CPU farms, and procurement lead times stretch into years, the more interesting question has moved up a level: *of the capacity we pay for and energize, how much is actually producing work?*
</div>

The answer, drawn from operator interviews and measured facility data collected for this report, is uncomfortable. Across the 41 hyperscale and colocation sites sampled, the median site reports **31.4% of energized capacity as non-productive** at any given hour — paid for, powered on, drawing baseload power, and producing no useful compute.

<blockquote className="pullquote my-10 p-6 lg:p-8">
  <div className="font-display text-[20px] lg:text-[24px] leading-[1.4] text-[var(--forest-800)] italic">
    "We don't have a power problem. We have a coordination problem disguised as a power problem."
  </div>
  <cite className="block mt-4 text-[12px] text-[var(--ink-muted)] tracking-wide uppercase not-italic">
    — Director of Infrastructure, Tier 1 Hyperscaler (anonymized)
  </cite>
</blockquote>

The thesis of this report is that stranded capacity is not a single problem with a single fix. It is a family of nine distinct failure modes, each with its own signature, cost, and root cause — distributed across the facility's three physical layers. Until the industry has a common name for each, operators will continue to describe different phenomena with the same word, and capital will continue to be deployed against the wrong layer.

What follows is a proposed vocabulary. It is open, public, and intended for debate.

## 03 — Taxonomy overview

### Three layers, nine named failures.

The taxonomy is organized by the physical layer where the failure originates. Each layer has its own operators, instrumentation, and language. A failure at one layer often manifests as a symptom in the layer above — which is why the industry has historically attributed stranded capacity to the wrong cause.

## 04 — Methodology

### How the index was built.

The Stranded Capacity Index synthesizes three sources of evidence: measured facility telemetry, scheduler export logs, and structured operator interviews. Each failure mode in the taxonomy must be observable in at least two of the three sources to be retained.

<div className="grid sm:grid-cols-2 gap-6 mb-12">
  <StepCard num="01" title="Telemetry Ingestion" borderColor="border-[var(--forest-700)]">
    kW per PDU, inlet temperature per rack, and BMC power per node at 5-minute resolution across 41 sites for 18 months. A total of 4.2 GW of energized IT load was sampled.
  </StepCard>
  <StepCard num="02" title="Scheduler Exports" borderColor="border-[var(--gold-500)]">
    Orchestrator state snapshots (Kubernetes, Slurm, proprietary) at 1-minute resolution. Captures registered vs. schedulable nodes, queue depth, and affinity rule sets.
  </StepCard>
  <StepCard num="03" title="Operator Interviews" borderColor="border-[var(--forest-500)]">
    63 structured interviews across facility owners, IT architects, and workload teams. Each failure mode named in the taxonomy was validated against the working language used by operators.
  </StepCard>
  <StepCard num="04" title="Naming & Peer Review" borderColor="border-[var(--forest-800)]">
    Each named failure mode was reviewed by at least three operators from different organizations. Names were retained only when at least two-thirds agreed the term matched their working vocabulary.
  </StepCard>
</div>

<div className="cite-box p-5 text-[12px] text-[var(--ink-muted)] leading-[1.6] rounded-sm">
  <strong className="text-[var(--forest-800)]">Limitations.</strong> The sample biases toward large hyperscale and colocation operators; edge and enterprise sites are underrepresented. The headline 31.4% figure is a sample median, not a population estimate — confidence intervals are reported by failure mode in the downloadable dataset.
